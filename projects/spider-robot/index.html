Legged Robot from Raw Servo Packets

Winter project, Dec. 2019 –⁠ Mar. 2020: a legged robot on DYNAMIXEL servos, packets and kinematics worked out by hand

From Dec. 2019 to Mar. 2020, as an undergraduate researcher at IHLAB (Tech University of Korea), I worked on a legged “spider” robot driven by DYNAMIXEL servos as my winter project. Starting from an ATmega128 training board, I wrote the servo protocol byte by byte, worked out the kinematics for a 3-axis arm and then for the legs, and debugged the servo bus on an oscilloscope. The first goal in my December plan was a four-legged spider robot; by March it crawled and turned on three legs and a caster, open loop. The legs, the suction feet and the green controller PCB were the lab’s; the firmware, the kinematics, a half-duplex adapter, the RS-485 fixes and the final hand-wired controller are mine.

What this project shows

Protocol from the wire up

I wrote the DYNAMIXEL packets and checksums byte by byte in C, with no servo library, after soldering the half-duplex adapter they needed. A raw log of 318 status packets has 0 checksum failures.

Measured against ground truth

I measured leg poses with a ruler and traced foot paths on paper, before and after the redesign: the largest traced error fell from 25 to 2 mm. In December I had already checked servo goal positions against a protractor.

Root causes, found on the scope

A clock 15× too slow, a leg that glitched, angles that read 4016.192°: I traced each to its cause with the oscilloscope (a faulty MCU or its soldering, status replies colliding with commands, a floating receive line) and fixed that cause.

Dead ends on record

My talk of 30 Jan. 2020 lists the leg glitch’s cause as “not found”; the next talk found it. The final runs were open loop because present-angle reads kept failing, and the March talk says so.

The robot, March 2020

  • Three legs and a caster. Each leg has three joints: an EX-106+ hip, turned through a 2:1 belt, and two RX-64 servos, on an RS-485 bus at about 14 V. The final runs used three legs and a caster, and with the caster in place all legs could move at the same time.
  • Open loop, said plainly. In March, present-angle reads failed so often that I ran the robot without them, on commanded positions alone. The serial command interface, laid out in February, was 1 for forward, 2 for turn right and 3 for turn left.
  • My own controller, wired by hand. I rebuilt the controller on perfboard: an ATmega128 core module, a buck converter module, a Bluetooth module, a MAX485 RS-485 transceiver and four servo connectors, wired point to point underneath.
  • A bad wire, found by elimination. Only leg C moved jerkily, so I ruled out the code. A multimeter check of the circuit’s wiring found no fault and no short, so I concluded the wire itself was bad; after I replaced it, leg C moved smoothly.

Servo protocol, byte by byte

  • A half-duplex adapter first. The AX-12A sends and receives on one data line, so in December I soldered an adapter for it: a buffer IC on an SOIC breakout board, a 10 kΩ pull-up and a servo connector, with two port pins switching the direction.
  • Packets checked against a protractor. I built each instruction packet and its checksum, ~(ID + LEN + INST + params), by hand. Goal positions 0x0300, 0x0200 and 0x0100 should turn the horn to 225°, 150° and 75°, and I photographed each one against a protractor.
  • Replies logged raw. I checked the status packets on the oscilloscope and logged the raw bytes: 318 packets, every checksum valid. Then came Sync Write, one broadcast packet for several servos with no reply, and a mirror demo in which one servo’s present angle became another’s goal.
  • From one servo to a 3-axis arm. I solved the arm’s 2-D inverse kinematics with the law of cosines, fixing one angle to remove the redundancy of three joints. Commanded angles of 150, 145.681 and 183.683° read back as 149.723, 145.621 and 183.125°: within 0.56°, less than two steps of the servo’s 0.293° resolution.

Faults found on the oscilloscope

  • The lab’s board, populated by me. The 140 × 90 mm controller PCB was the lab’s. I populated it with the ATmega128 and a MAX485 transceiver, added a buck converter on a perfboard daughterboard and wired in the 14 V supply. When 14 V later reached the MCU and destroyed it, I soldered the circuit again with only the parts in use, to avoid shorts.
  • A clock 15× too slow. On that board, delay_us(10) took 150 µs. From the delay loop’s cycle count I estimated a clock of about 1 MHz instead of 14.7456 MHz. I re-soldered the crystal, re-set the fuse bits and probed both crystal pins; the crystal oscillated near its nominal frequency, so I replaced the MCU, and delay_us(10) then took 10.0 µs.
  • Replies colliding with commands. The first leg glitched now and then, and my talk of 30 Jan. listed the cause as not found. On the scope, each servo answers a write with a status reply, about 700 µs later by the slide’s note, while my code sent the next command after only 100 µs. Setting Status Return Level 2 → 1, so that servos answer only reads, stopped the glitches.
  • RS-485 at the physical layer. On transmit, my code released the driver enable as soon as the transmit buffer was empty, before the last byte had left; the fix waits 200 µs before releasing it, with interrupts off. On receive, the MCU’s RX line floats while the MAX485 transmits, because its receiver is disabled then, and angles read 4016.192° or random values. A 10 kΩ pull-up on that line made the readings follow the command (13.425° ↔ 60.748°); I also added a termination resistor across A and B against reflections.

Kinematics, checked on paper

  • Joints that finish together. For each move the firmware read the present angles, set each joint’s speed in proportion to the angle it still had to turn, queued the three goals with REG_WRITE and started them with a single ACTION, so that all three joints would finish together.
  • A drift found, and its cause. A ruler confirmed single poses (147 against 150 mm, 70 against 69 mm), but during moves the foot that should have stayed 150 mm from the body traced 145 and 125 mm. Splitting a move by joint angles cannot hold a Cartesian distance, so I rewrote it: forward kinematics gives the current point, small Cartesian steps lead to the target, and inverse kinematics runs at every step.
  • A conversion bug, found by plotting. I logged x, y and z at every step and plotted them. In one step x jumped from 180 to 208.074 and y from 84 to 105.588, which pointed to a wrong conversion; after the fix, x held near 180 mm while y and z traced clean trapezoids.
  • Re-traced after the redesign. The foot then kept 179 and 178.5 mm against a target of 180, the hip turned 29.7° against 30°, and the sideways reach was 234 and 123 mm against 235 and 125: every trace within 1–2 mm and 0.3°.

한국어 요약

IHLAB 학부연구생으로 2019년 12월부터 2020년 3월까지 진행한 겨울방학 프로젝트입니다. ATmega128에서 다이나믹셀 패킷과 체크섬을 한 바이트씩 직접 짜고, 3축 로봇팔과 다리의 기구학을 풀었습니다. 12월 계획의 첫 목표는 다리 4개짜리 거미로봇이었고, 3월에는 다리 3개와 캐스터로 기어가며 방향을 트는 로봇을 개루프로 구동했습니다. 다리와 흡착 발, 초록색 제어 PCB는 연구실 장비이고, 펌웨어와 기구학 계산, 반이중 어댑터, RS-485 통신 수정, 만능기판 제어기는 제 작업입니다.

결과는 각도기와 자, 오실로스코프로 직접 재어 확인했습니다. 15배 느린 클럭은 MCU 교체로, 다리 오작동은 상태 패킷 충돌을 찾아 반환 레벨 변경으로, 엉뚱한 각도값은 풀업 저항과 종단 저항을 달아 고쳤습니다. 관절각을 나눠 움직일 때 25 mm까지 벌어지던 발 위치 오차는 직교좌표 보간 뒤 1–2 mm 이내로 줄었고, 마지막 구동이 현재 각도를 읽지 못한 개루프였다는 점도 그대로 기록했습니다.
